# The Seventh Proof / Story Outline

## Backstory

### **Concept**

**The Seventh Proof* is a nonlinear narrative mystery game and modern reimagining of Mikhail Bulgakov’s *The Master and Margarita*, set in London during the seven days before a bitter UK General Election.

The city is splitting itself in two. On every screen and street corner, rival political movements denounce each other as the source of national ruin. Beneath that public war, however, both sides are secretly fed by the same criminal pipeline: a laundering network running through elite art institutions, religious charities, extremist cells, and political dark money.

Into this city comes Professor Woland (The Devil) and his entourage: a world-famous mentalist booked to perform a one-night gala at the Royal Alliance Theatre on election eve. His arrival triggers a chain of disappearances, impossible crimes, supernatural visitations, and grotesque events. But Woland has not come to corrupt London. London has already done that work itself. He has come to expose the distance between public masks and private truth.

Players experience the mystery through several playable characters whose stories unfold out of order: Kenneth Fletcher, a grieving detective drawn into the case of a missing theatre director; Arthur Pennington, the corrupt patron whose disappearance begins the chain; Margaret Spenceley, a woman desperate to find her missing lover; Zoey Fernandez, a witness trapped inside a secret life; and Michael Astor, a journalist whose hidden dossier may prove what really happened during a terrorist attack.

Gameplay combines environmental investigation, puzzle solving, dialogue pressure, clue reconstruction, and shifting perspective. Each character knows only part of the truth. The player’s task is to assemble those partial truths into a coherent account of the conspiracy.

At its core, the game is a mystery about truth under theatrical disguise: political masks, charitable masks, religious masks, romantic masks, and finally the Devil’s own mask as entertainer. The horror is supernatural, but the crime is human.

### **Theme**

**The Spine.** Unlike the novel, which defended the concept of "faith" against atheism and corrupt bourgeoisie of the Soviet Union, this story aims its satire at a city that has hollowed itself out to the bone by dogmatism, hypocrisy and cowardice. Woland does not arrive in London to corrupt anyone. He arrives, as he did in Moscow, to take inventory, and finds a civilization that has already corrupted itself; Its institutions believe in nothing but their own interest and they live only by feeding the fanatics and extremists. They wear every creed at once because each is only a costume the money and power arrive in. This is the story's central, Bulgakovian message: *the Devil is more honest than the institutions, because the Devil, at least, does not lie about what he is.* He is the only sincere thing in a city of masks.

The dual-extreme political conflict of the election is the story's *setting*. It is the noise the city makes while the real rot is happening underneath. The satire is not "both political sides are bad"; it defends a specific value, and it aims beneath politics at something older: sincerity toward the truth.

**The Cardinal Sin: the gap between truth and performance..* Every guilty party in the story betrays truth in one of three ways:

1. **Dogmatism:** Believers who have stopped testing their beliefs against reality: party loyalists, religious extremists, ideological commentators, street movements, donors, and ordinary citizens who would rather inherit a ready-made enemy than endure uncertainty. Dogmatism is not faith or conviction. It is the refusal to examine them.
2. **Hypocrisy:** The powerful, who wear beliefs they privately betray. Arthur Pennington sells culture and democracy while living as an aristocrat of appetite. Yusuf Tariq collects donations in the language of God, nation, and compassion while treating faith as a laundering instrument. The campaigns turn tragedy into opposite slogans while feeding from the same concealed pipeline.
3. **Cowardice** People who know enough to doubt and act, and yet choose comfort anyway: editors who do not publish, officials who bury files, citizens who accept the convenient version, and fanatics who fear the collapse of the stories they shout in public.

Dogmatism, hypocrisy, and cowardice are the same crime: the distance between the self that is shown and truth. Against it, the story defends a single value: *sincerity towards the truth*. Woland's function throughout is to collapse that distance, to drag the true face out of the shadows and into the light. To this end, he plays with three persons throughout the story, who still are paying a ruinous price for caring about one real thing, each is the sincere inversion of the machines hypocrisy: Truth, Love and Courage.

**The Verdict.** Woland's journey ends not in punishment but in a **choice.** He can grant the worn-out and the finished their ultimate *peace*: release or an exit from a city beyond their power to save. Who will choose which?! 

### **The Election**

London, a week before a contentious UK General Election. The city is a powder keg of political and ideological tension. Two factions dominate every screen, speaker, and street corner:

- **The Concord Party**, led by **Diane Sterling**: Polished, technocratic, labor/liberal, preaching unity and open borders, while quietly dismantling everything local and national; the smiling face of globalism that treats dissent as ignorance.
- **The New Albion Movement** **(NAM)**, led by Edward Thorne: A firebrand promising national rebirth, flirting openly with religious hardliners and not-so-openly with street militias; nostalgia weaponized into resentment.

Unknown to the public, and to each candidate's fanatics, *both campaigns secretly drink from the same financial well.* Their dark money flows through the same laundering pipelines: The One Soul Charity Foundation and The Royal Alliance Theatre Associations being two of those. The two parties screaming at each other across every divide are, beneath the floorboards, business partners. This is the central corruption the story exposes.



### **Arthur Pennington and Yusuf Tariq**

**Arthur Pennington:** director of the Royal Alliance Theatre Associations, and one of London's wealthiest men. To the public he is the great democratizer of culture: a celebrated patron of the arts, a tireless Concord and labor party fundraiser, a self-styled man of the people who never tires of reminding everyone he rose from nothing. He gives the speeches that make donors weep and the galas that make the papers. Behind the curtain though, he is a pure appetite; A connoisseur of fine drink, finer art, and women he keeps carefully apart from one another, ruling his fortress penthouse like a Caesar who has confused taste with virtue. He genuinely loves the theatre; that is his one true thing, and it is precisely what he prostituted, turning a temple of art into a laundromat for dirty money. Vain, theatrical, and addicted to his own performance of goodness, Arthur adores riddles and locks; He hides everything, because a man with this much to hide learns to enjoy the hiding.

**Yusuf Tariq** director of the One Soul Charity Foundation, and Arthur's partner and long-term friend in everything that matters. Where Arthur is flame, Yusuf is frost. He is the city's model of sober virtue: devout, soft-spoken, austere in dress and habit, a bridge-builder who collects in the name of God and nation and is photographed beside bishops, imams, and ministers of every stripe. He believes in none of it. Faith, to Yusuf, is simply the most efficient currency ever devised; It arrives untaxed, unquestioned, and wrapped in gratitude; And One Soul is the cathedral he built to launder it, quietly feeding the very extremist cells whose fury keeps the donations flowing. Disciplined, patient, and genuinely frightening in his calm, he is the strategist of the pair: Arthur dreams up the spectacle, Yusuf does the planning. He leaves no fingerprints, because he has trained himself to want nothing he could be caught wanting.



### **The Theatre Attack**

A year before the events of the game, masked gunmen stormed a Royal Alliance branch playhouse during an evening performance of Goethe’s *Faust*. They entered mid-performance, when the house lights were low and the audience was still enough to mistake the first seconds for part of the show.

Then the shooting began.

Several people were killed in the auditorium. Others were taken through the service corridors and vanished into the night. Among the dead were Kenneth Fletcher’s wife and five-year-old daughter. Among the missing was investigative journalist **Michael Astor**, officially recorded only as “unaccounted for.”

A fringe religious-extremist cell claimed responsibility in a confused, theatrical message. The claim was enough for the press, enough for the politicians, and enough for counter-terror command to take the case away from ordinary detectives. But the details never fully settled: no ransom demand followed, no hostages were recovered, no clear mastermind was named, and the investigation slowly disappeared into sealed files and institutional embarrassment.

London grieved, raged, argued, and moved on. A year later, both campaigns still invoke the attack from their podiums. Edward Thorne uses it as proof of national decay, open borders, and the enemy within. Diane Sterling uses it as proof of where hatred and extremism inevitably lead. Neither campaign understands that the same hidden pipeline has profited from both versions of the story.

**What actually happened (hidden canon):** the attack was never terrorism at all, but a false flag built to look like one; A single, cold-blooded operation engineered to pay several dividends at once. Arthur Pennington and Yusuf Tariq (the managing directors of the Royal Alliance Theatre and the One Soul Charity Foundation) designed it together and outsourced the execution to a religious extremist cell, one of several quietly nourished through the One Soul money pipeline; Fanatics who believed they were striking a blow for their own cause and never suspected they were only instruments. Timed to the very week both candidates launched their campaigns, the massacre was meant to achieve three things at once:

- **Inflame the extremes.** A slaughter at the theatre handed both factions their sharpest weapon: Thorne could brandish it as proof of national decay, open borders and the enemy within; Sterling, as proof of where hatred and division inevitably lead. The same blood fuelled both campaigns...and since both, beneath the floorboards, drank from the same well, every pound of outrage donated to either side flowed home through the very pipeline that had paid for the bullets. The conspiracy had manufactured the polarisation it profited from.
- **Cripple the police.** An unsolved mass-casualty attack, its investigation swallowed upward into counter-terror commands and quietly steered into the ground, left the force humiliated, overstretched and distrusted on the eve of an election both candidates would fight on the ruins of "law and order." A blamed and broken police was a police that could not afford to look too closely at anything else.
- **The Abduction.** The real prize in the audience that night was one man: the journalist **Michael Astor**, who had come too close to the hidden pipeline; And who Arthur and Yusuf planned to capture alive.

It was meant to be brutal, and it was; But it ran even bloodier than its architects had modelled, the cell killing more freely and more widely than ordered. The atrocity that now fuels every podium in London is, in truth, the conspiracy's single most profitable transaction and its bloodiest piece of bookkeeping...and Michael Astor walked out of it alive, into a cage.

### **Michael Astor and Margaret Spenceley**

**Michael Astor**: One of our protagonists. Investigative journalist and writer; byline *M. Astor*. Two years before the story begins, he started pulling on a single loose thread: the impossible finances of the Royal Alliance Theatre Association and its business partner, the One Soul Charity Foundation. Curious, stubborn, and working alone (he trusted no editor with it), he spent a year mapping the pipeline: the donations, the fake art commissions, the crypto-currency wash and the political accounts at both ends. He compiled it all into his personal laptop and hid it. Then, one evening at the theatre, the gunmen came. Officially, Michael has been *"unaccounted for"* since the attack; Not among the identified dead, not a confirmed hostage. The police filed him into the void between categories and stopped looking.

(Hidden canon: Michael is alive; Committed under a false name and a false diagnosis in a private psychiatric clinic funded by the One Soul Charity Foundation. The clinic is literally his cage; his records are sealed behind "patient's privacy." He is kept sedated, discredited, and alive for exactly one reason: only he knows where his laptop is hidden. They cannot kill him until they have it; he will not give it up, because it is the only thing keeping him alive. A year of this stalemate has nearly unmade him, but he spends every lucid hour plotting his escape, by any means he can find.

**Margaret Spenceley**: Another protagonist. Michael's partner and the love of his life. She has spent a year on cold trails: vigils, missing-person bureaucracy, dwindling police sympathy, friends who gently suggest a grief counselor. What she has instead of evidence is an unshakeable, irrational certainty that Michael is alive and that he is somewhere in London. She is right, and this certainty is precisely the desperation that leads her towards Woland.

### **Kenneth Fletcher**

Our main protagonist. A disciplined, brilliant investigator with a sharp mind for political and philosophical questions. He is an official detective in the force, but he also maintains unofficial connections around the country who feed him leads and quiet, off-the-books cases for extra money.

His wife and and his 5-year old daughter were in the audience at the branch playhouse that night, a year ago. They did not come home. And because the case was terrorism, it never belonged to men like Kenneth: it vanished upward into counter-terror commands, classified briefings, and sealed files. A man who interrogates evidence for a living was never permitted to interrogate the evidence of his own family's death. The grief broke him; the unanswered questions keep him standing. He has grown utterly weary and disillusioned with everything that goes around him, specially politics, which he was once very passionate about. To him, Sterling's and Thorne's voices are the same noise played in different keys...and he cannot stand that his family's murder has become a talking point in both of their mouths.

### **Professor Woland and His Entourage**

Days before the election, strange posters appear suddenly across the city: **"PROFESSOR WOLAND & THE SEVENTH PROOF: AN EVENING OF THE IMPOSSIBLE"**. A world-famous foreign mentalist and illusionist, no surname given, no past traceable, has been contracted for a one-night-only gala performance at the Royal Alliance's flagship theatre on **election eve**.

Woland's retinue, each with a mundane cover and an inhuman truth:

- **Koroviev**: Woland's lanky, grinning "tour manager and interpreter," in a cheap checked suit and cracked pince-nez. He does the talking, the paperwork, and the lying; his jokes have a way of coming true.
- **Azazello**: "Head of security." Short, broad, red-haired, one protruding fang and a wall-eye. The enforcer; where physical violence is required, Azazello attends to it.
- **Hella**: The Professor's "personal assistant," redheaded and unsettlingly beautiful, handling hospitality and visitors. Visitors she handles alone are rarely seen again.
- **Behemoth**: The star attraction of the show: an enormous black cat who walks on his hind legs, drinks whisky, smokes cigars, and is advertised as "a marvel of training." He is no one's pet, and his sense of humour is lethal.
- **Abaddon**: On no payroll and no poster. A pale, eyeless figure who is never introduced and never speaks; he simply *appears*; Glimpsed in a reflection, at the end of a corridor, across a street, only when a death is imminent. *(Design note: Abaddon functions as a recurring visual omen for the player; once the pattern is learned, his appearance becomes the game's most dreadful clue.)*

### **The Inciting Case**

It all began with one of those unofficial leads. On a Sunday evening, one of his connections calls Kenneth: a man named *Arthur Pennington* has disappeared. The name strikes a strangely familiar chord to Kenneth. The informant explains that an anonymous caller reported Arthur missing from home and office for days adding, ominously, that *"he will be disposed of soon..."* Arthur Pennington is the director of the Royal Alliance Theatre Association, one of London's wealthiest men; therefor, the case could be very lucrative. But it isn't the money that decides it. Upon hearing the theatre's name, *that* theatre, Kenneth accepts on the spot, and begins his secret investigation at Arthur's residence: the penthouse of the famous Silver Tower, No. 50 Godolphin Street.

*(Canon note: Kenneth does not know Michael Astor exists, and does not suspect the attack was anything but what the news said. His "lucrative missing-person case" is, though he doesn't know it yet, his family's case. The man who vanished from the penthouse is the man who ordered the attack. The outline must distribute clues to this fairly across the acts before any reveal.)*

## Act 1

### 1.1 The Call and the Tower

The act opens with a comic-style sequence: a late-night phone call from **Frank Briggs**, one of Kenneth's off-the-books informants. Briggs lays out the case: Arthur Pennington, director of the Royal Alliance Theatre Associations, missing for days; an anonymous caller warning that he "will be disposed of soon"; and the kind of payday that could put a broken man back on his feet. Briggs gives one strict instruction: enter the Silver Tower at exactly **11:30 p.m.**, when, he promises, the building's security and lobby staff will be gone for one hour. When Kenneth asks how he could possibly arrange that, Briggs only deflects. Kenneth takes the case anyway; Not for the money, but because of the theatre's name, in which he lost his wife and daughter only a year ago. *(Hidden canon: "Briggs" is Behemoth, mimicking the real informant's voice; the entire case is a bait, laid by Woland's crew to draw Kenneth in.)*

Gameplay begins as Kenneth steps into the Silver Tower lobby at half past eleven. Everything suggests the staff were here only moments ago...a half-finished cup of tea, a chair still turned, a visitor log open mid-signature...and yet the place is utterly deserted, with no sign of how Briggs emptied it. ( Kenneth to himself : "How the hell did he do that?"). To reach the penthouse, the player must solve two puzzles within the lobby environment: recovering the **security code for the elevator**, and locating the **keycard to Arthur Pennington's penthouse**.

With both in hand, Kenneth rides up through an increasingly silent, wrong-feeling tower, and enters Arthur Pennington's Penthouse.

### 1.2 The Penthouse

Inside, the penthouse is immaculate ; Modern, minimalist, expensively artistic, with a discreet sensual streak; And there is not a single trace of Arthur. At this point Kenneth can access only the public wing: the entrance hall and the home office. The lived-in calm is broken by a single headline anomaly: a few deep claw marks gouged into the walls, like the fang-strokes of an enormous cat...faint in the hall, and most violent around one heavy, reinforced door.

Everything else is sealed. Arthur is passionate about security, and the private quarters, the bedroom, his collection, the rooms where his appetites live, run on a biometric system Kenneth cannot defeat tonight. Kenneth's task is to explore the two open rooms and read what Arthur left lying about. The central puzzle is a physical one, built to flatter its owner's love of riddles: a theatrical puzzle-box mechanism in the office, an antique model stage, which, manipulated in the right sequence, releases an electric safe. Inside are documents exposing the tight cooperation between the Royal Alliance Theatre and a second body, the **One Soul Charity Foundation**, and naming **Yusuf Tariq** as One Soul's director. The papers establish that the two are deeply entwined, but not yet how or why;

Three quieter threads are planted for the attentive player. First, a peculiar business card embossed with a single golden **'W'**, found in the office...no name, no number. Second, subtle evidence of Arthur's double life: two different perfumes, two sets of intimate gifts, a discreet second phone, hinting at two women he is keeping hidden from each other. And third, buried among the safe's papers, a year-old payment record...an absurd sum, the payee redacted to the initials **"M.A / Final"**; Paper-clipped to a press cutting about the theatre attack; Kenneth misreads it bitterly as charity hush-money paid to a victim's family. *(Hidden canon: this is the buried Michael Astor seed...invisible now, devastating on the reveal and on replay.)* The case board updates, and Kenneth's next destination is set: The Royal Alliance Theatre Main Building.

### 1.3 Arthur: The Night It Happened

We jump back three nights, to the evening of Arthur Pennington's disappearance, and take his role. This is the payoff to the locked penthouse: the player now moves freely through the private wing Kenneth could not reach...the bedroom, the collection, the rooms where Arthur's appetites live...seeing the true face behind the public mask. Arthur wakes long after midnight, savagely hungover, to his phone ringing in the dark. It is Zoey, his chief secretary, asking for Professor Woland's number ahead of tomorrow's meeting, and insisting it is in the contract they drafted that very morning. Arthur remembers no Woland and no contract, but his skull is splitting, and he goes to look. *(Hidden canon: the call is Behemoth, mimicking Zoey's voice; the real Zoey never rang.)*

As the player guides him through the fortress in search of his briefcase, the environment lays Arthur's hypocrisy bare: the self-styled man of the people drowning in obscene luxury, the traces of two women kept carefully apart, the locked cabinet of trophies, the quiet machinery of laundered wealth. He finds the briefcase, and inside it, the contract; Woland's, signed in Arthur's own hand, on a document he has never seen. The paper is warm to the touch.

Then the penthouse turns on him. Doors lock by themselves, the lights stutter, and the rooms warp into a vision of his crime: the paintings bleed into a burning playhouse, the reek of smoke and cordite floods the halls, and the screams of the dead from a year ago rise through the walls; The massacre, replayed as accusation. Arthur denies it, rationalises, flees toward the bedroom, and freezes. By the window stands an enormous black cat, upright on its hind legs, a cigar in one paw and a glass of whisky in the other, regarding the city with lazy contempt. Behemoth turns; its claws rake the reinforced door...the very gouges Kenneth will find three nights later...as Arthur's vision goes black and he collapses. *(The player grasps that Arthur is guilty of the atrocity; the why...the false flag, the abduction, the families destroyed...stays hidden.)*

### 1.4 Margaret: Stranger in the Park

On the same rainy night, we shift to a new playable character: Margaret Spenceley. She sits alone on a bench in Hyde Park, huddled under a small shelter, while the city seethes around her...the pouring rain, the distant clash of protesters, police sirens, and the amplified voices of the two candidates warring over the park's loudspeakers. A year of searching has hollowed her out; she feels nothing for the politics drowning London. *(Backstory: Margaret is Michael Astor's partner, sustained by an irrational, unshakeable certainty that he is alive and somewhere in this city.)*

A stranger seems to step out of the rain itself and sits beside her. An interactive dialogue begins, where the player chooses Margaret's tone...bitter, guarded, or pleading; The choices shape her character and his amusement, never the outcome. He asks her view of the election, then dismantles the whole spectacle with terrifying charm, exposing the hypocrisies, frauds, and shared secret financing of both factions, citing specifics no ordinary citizen could possibly know...and which Kenneth's investigation will later prove true. For one night, the Devil is the only honest voice in London.

Then he turns personal, and asks what she is truly looking for. Margaret tells him the thing she tells no one: that she is searching for the man she loves, lost a year ago, and that she knows, against all reason, he is still here. The stranger offers her a pact; He will help her find him, if she will perform a series of services for him, the nature of which he refuses to name. Overwhelmed by the impossible power she senses in him, and too desperate to refuse the only person who has ever told her she is right, Margaret accepts. *(Theme: Margaret is the story's sincere Love, the inversion of the city's hypocrisy...which is exactly what makes her useful to Woland, and what he will spend.)*

## Act 2

\-\>Returning to the present, Kenneth decides to investigate Arthur’s office in the Royal Alliance building next. Unable to do so officially or in broad daylight, he must find a way to infiltrate the theatre at midnight and locate the management floor. Upon reaching the floor, Kenneth is struck by an unnatural silence. It isn't merely quiet; the place feels dead. There is no hum of servers, no distant traffic noise, no night watchman. It is then that he spots the first bloodstain on the expensive carpet. Following the trail with his flashlight, he discovers more disturbing signs: deep, long scratches on the wallpaper and, in some places, massive parallel gouges, as if made by the fangs of a giant cat or tiger.

\-\>Inside Arthur’s luxurious office, and after hacking into his computer, Kenneth’s investigation peels back the first layer of real-world corruption. He discovers a hidden folder and some encrypted files on Arthur’s personal computer. The evidence is undeniable: Arthur and Yusuf are partners in a colossal money-laundering scheme. The "One Soul" foundation and the "Royal Alliance" theatres receive millions of pounds in religious and public donations, which are then funnelled into fake art commissions and cryptocurrency trades, effectively laundering funds for both major political factions in the election.

\-\>Furthermore, the computer exposes a personal fiasco: a locked folder of confidential emails reveals that Arthur was having affairs with his assistant, Zoey Fernandez (a married woman), and the theatre’s financial manager, Rebecca Evans. Finishing his digital search, Kenneth returns to the hallway and spots a trail of fresh blood leading toward the staff break room. He pushes the door open. His flashlight beam cuts through the darkness to reveal a horrific scene. A headless corpse lies in the center of the room. On the counter, displayed like a grotesque trophy, sits the severed head of Rebecca Evans, her face frozen in a silent scream of terror...

\-\>We now go back to the previous night, playing the role of Zoey Fernandez, inside the deserted Royal Alliance Theatre. She and the financial manager, Rebecca, are desperately trying to locate Arthur, who has been missing for days. Crucially, we learn here that the phone call Arthur received earlier, which we thought was from Zoey, was a supernatural fabrication by Behemoth; the real Zoey never spoke to him that night and now she is consumed by anxiety. She fears not just for Arthur’s safety, but that his disappearance will expose their affair.   
Unknown to Zoey, Rebecca is also secretly involved with Arthur, and the two women are collaborating to find their shared lover. After a tense discussion, Rebecca volunteers to check the local pubs and bars Arthur frequently visits, leaving Zoey alone in the building. Seizing the opportunity, Zoey decides to act on her growing suspicions about Rebecca. Zoey must solve complex security puzzles to bypass the locks on Rebecca’s office so that she can enter. Inside, she uncovers clues suggesting Rebecca's own secret involvement with Arthur, deepening Zoey’s paranoia, confusion and anger.

\-\>Upon returning to her own office, Zoey finds that Rebecca has returned much sooner than expected. Rebecca is sitting motionless behind a desk, her face obscured by heavy shadows, behaving with an unnerving, rigid and weird stillness. An interactive dialogue sequence initiates here. We can choose how Zoey questions Rebecca about her search. Initially, Rebecca claims she found Arthur drunk at a pub and sent him home in a taxi. However, as we press her with more specific questions, her story begins to fracture; the contradictions grow wilder and the lies more absurd with every response. As the interrogation continues, Rebecca leans forward, bringing her face out of the shadows.   
We see that her face is battered, dented, and wounded, yet she speaks as if nothing is wrong. Suddenly, the atmosphere shifts violently into horror: the lights in the office abruptly cut out, plunging the room into darkness. In the flickering failing light, Zoey sees a massive, frozen hand reaching through the window. Simultaneously, Rebecca’s head detaches and slides unnaturally off her neck. The scene cuts to black, echoing with the sound of a woman’s bloody scream along with the shriek of a giant cat.
